1% better every day: the math and how to measure it

Get 1% better every day for a year and you end up 37 times better. The calculation is correct. The promise is not quite. Here is what the math really says, what to take from it and how to measure today against yesterday in five minutes.

Published 25 November 2026 · 5 min read

The math behind 1% better every day

The idea was made famous by the book Atomic Habits. Take the number 1 and grow it by 1% a day. Because each day builds on the day before, the growth compounds:

  • After 30 days: 1.01 to the power of 30 is about 1.35, or 35% better.
  • After 90 days: about 2.45 times.
  • After 365 days: about 37.8 times.
  • The other direction: lose 1% a day for a year and you are left with about 0.03, which is almost nothing.

Without compounding, 1% of your starting point added each day gives 3.65 on top after a year, so about 4.65 times. The gap between 4.65 and 37.8 is all compounding.

Where the math breaks

People are not savings accounts. If you bench 100 pounds today, you will not bench 3,780 pounds next year. Real progress looks different in four ways:

  • It slows down. Beginners improve fast. Later, each step costs more.
  • It is bumpy. Sleep, stress and illness move your results by far more than 1% from one day to the next.
  • It is often invisible. Strength, fitness and skill show up in weeks, not overnight.
  • Some of it does not compound. Doing ten push-ups today does not automatically make tomorrow's eleven.

So drop the 37. Keep the part that holds up: small actions, repeated on most days, in the same direction, add up to more than rare big efforts. It also works in reverse, and that is the part people forget. A bad day costs little. A bad direction costs a lot.

What 1% looks like in real life

One percent of a 16-hour waking day is just under 10 minutes. That is a useful way to see it. You are looking for ten minutes of better:

  • Gym: one more rep on one set than last time.
  • Food: one meal with 30 g of protein that would have been a snack.
  • Study or work: ten more minutes before the first phone check.
  • Sleep: lights out ten minutes earlier.
  • Phone: ten minutes less scrolling.
  • Reading: five pages instead of none.
  • People: one call you would have put off.

Pick one or two of these, not all seven. If you try to improve everything daily, you run out of room within a week.

How to measure today vs yesterday

"Better" needs a number, otherwise you go by mood. Here is a simple score you can keep on paper.

Step 1: list your daily actions

Choose four to six things you control. Use actions, not results. "Trained 45 minutes" belongs on the list. "Lost half a pound" does not, because the scale moves for reasons you cannot see.

Step 2: give them points by difficulty

Easy is 1 point, medium 2, hard 3, brutal 5. Otherwise a day of five easy tasks looks better than a day with one brutal exam session, and you start chasing the easy ones.

Step 3: add the things you avoided

Give 2 points for each thing you cut out and stayed away from today.

Step 4: compare

Add it up in the evening and compare with yesterday. Example: yesterday 12 points, today 13. The change is 1 divided by 12, or about 8% up. Tomorrow you have a clear number to beat.

Today vs yesterday in five minutes

  • Four to six actions listed, rated easy to brutal
  • One or two things I stayed away from
  • Points added up before bed
  • Percent change from yesterday written down
  • One sentence: why was it higher or lower?
  • The first task for tomorrow chosen

Why yesterday is the right opponent

Your best day ever is too far away, and other people live a different life. Yesterday is close. You know exactly what happened, you had the same job and the same body, and the gap is usually small enough to close. It also resets every night: a terrible Tuesday makes Wednesday easy to win, which is when you need a win.

There is one risk. If you only look at a single day, you may chase numbers when you should rest. That is why you also need a longer view.

Look at the week too

Every Sunday, write down the average of your seven scores. Single days are noise and the weekly average is the signal. Three rules keep it fair:

  1. Plan rest days as part of the score. A walk, stretching and early sleep are the tasks for that day. A rest day done well is a full day.
  2. Expect a ceiling. Once your days are full, the score will flatten. Then make a task harder instead of adding more tasks.
  3. Two weeks down means change something. Less sleep? Too many tasks? A distraction that came back? Fix that one cause.

A 30-day 1% better plan

  • Week 1: only record. Do your normal life and write the score each evening, so you know your baseline.
  • Week 2: improve one action by ten minutes or one rep.
  • Week 3: keep it and cut one thing. Short videos are a strong candidate. Our guide on how to stop doomscrolling shows how.
  • Week 4: move your hardest task to the first slot of the day and see what happens to the score.

On day 30, compare the average of week 4 with week 1. If it rose, you have proof in your own handwriting. If it did not, read the one-sentence notes and you will probably see the cause. For the habits side of it, continue with how to be disciplined.

FAQ

What is 1% better every day for a year?

Mathematically, 1.01 to the power of 365 is about 37.8, so 37.8 times the starting value. In real life progress slows down and is uneven, so treat it as a picture of consistency and not as a forecast.

Is 1% better every day realistic?

Not as an exact daily gain forever. Making one small improvement on most days is realistic, and over months it adds up to clear changes.

How do I measure 1% better?

List four to six daily actions, give them points by difficulty, add them up each evening and compare with yesterday. Check the weekly average too, because single days vary.

What does 1% of a day mean?

One percent of 24 hours is 14.4 minutes. One percent of a 16-hour waking day is about 10 minutes.

What if today was worse than yesterday?

That is normal and will happen often. Note the reason, do the basics tomorrow and watch the weekly average rather than one day.

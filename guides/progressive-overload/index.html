Progressive overload, explained with real numbers

Your muscles adapt to what you ask of them, so to keep getting stronger you have to ask a little more over time. That is progressive overload. The hard part is not the idea; it is knowing exactly when and how much to add. Double progression answers both.

Published 26 November 2026 · 5 min read

What progressive overload means

Progressive overload means gradually increasing the demand on a muscle across weeks. If you lift the same weight for the same reps every session, your body has no reason to change. If you do a bit more over time, it adapts by getting stronger and, with enough food and sleep, bigger.

"A bit more" can mean several things:

  • More weight for the same reps.
  • More reps with the same weight.
  • More sets across the week.
  • Better form or range of motion, such as squatting deeper with control.
  • Same work in less rest time, which matters less for strength but still counts.

For most people, the first two are the ones to focus on. They are easy to measure and they drive most of the progress.

Double progression: the simplest method

Double progression means you progress in two steps: first reps, then weight. Every exercise gets a rep range, such as 8-12. You pick a weight you can lift for the bottom of that range with good form, then:

  1. Each session, try to add a rep to one or more sets.
  2. When you hit the top of the range on every working set, add the smallest weight jump next time.
  3. Drop back to the bottom of the range with the new weight and build up again.

Example: dumbbell row, 3 sets of 8-12

  • Week 1, 25 kg: 10, 9, 8
  • Week 2, 25 kg: 11, 10, 9
  • Week 3, 25 kg: 12, 11, 10
  • Week 4, 25 kg: 12, 12, 12 (top of range on every set)
  • Week 5, 27.5 kg: 9, 8, 8 (new weight, start again)

Example: squat, 3 sets of 5-8

  • Session 1, 80 kg: 7, 6, 6
  • Session 2, 80 kg: 8, 7, 6
  • Session 3, 80 kg: 8, 8, 8
  • Session 4, 82.5 kg: 6, 6, 5

The rule is simple enough that you never have to decide in the gym. You only need to know what you did last time.

How much weight to add

Use the smallest jump your gym allows. For big barbell lifts (squat, deadlift, bench, row), that is usually 2.5 kg / 5 lb. For overhead press and smaller dumbbell lifts, 1-2 kg / 2.5-5 lb. For isolation moves like lateral raises and curls, the jumps are big relative to the weight, so stay in the rep range longer and use a wider range, like 12-20.

Bigger jumps feel faster for a week and then stall you for a month. Small and steady wins.

Progressive overload, every session

  • Look up last session's weight and reps before you start
  • Warm up with 2-3 lighter sets on the first big lift
  • Stop each working set 1-2 reps before failure, with clean form
  • Log every working set
  • Add weight only when every set hit the top of the range
  • Keep the same exercise for at least 6-8 weeks

What to do when you stall

A stall is when you fail to add a rep or weight on a lift for two or three sessions in a row. Before you change the program, check the basics, because they cause most stalls:

  • Sleep: are you getting 7-9 hours most nights?
  • Food: are you eating enough protein (around 1.6 g per kg of body weight) and not in a big calorie deficit?
  • Effort: are you stopping sets far from failure out of habit?
  • Consistency: did you actually train the planned days?

If those are fine, try in this order: drop the weight about 10% and build back up with double progression; widen the rep range (5-8 becomes 6-10); or swap the exercise for a close variation, such as incline press for flat bench, for one block. A lighter week every 6-10 weeks also helps joints and motivation recover.

Progressive overload in a deficit

If you are trying to lose fat, progress will be slower. That is normal. Your goal in a deficit is to keep your strength, and any progress is a bonus. Keep the rep ranges, accept adding a rep instead of weight, and make sure your deficit is moderate. Our guide on how many calories to eat to lose weight helps you set it.

Track it or it did not happen

Double progression only works if you know last session's numbers. Memory is not good enough across 15 exercises and three weeks. Write down weight and reps for every working set, either in a notebook or an app like LOCKDMAX, which fills in your next targets for you.

Then pick a program and stick with it. A push pull legs split and the 4-day Winter Arc workout plan both use double progression. If you have an injury or a medical condition, check with a doctor or physio before you start lifting heavier.

FAQ

What is progressive overload?

Gradually making your training harder over time, mainly by adding reps or weight, so your muscles keep a reason to get stronger.

How often should I increase weight?

With double progression, whenever you hit the top of the rep range on every working set. For beginners that can be every week or two; for experienced lifters it is slower.

Is adding reps progressive overload?

Yes. More reps with the same weight is more total work, and it is the first step of double progression before you add weight.

Can you do progressive overload with dumbbells at home?

Yes. Use wider rep ranges, slow the lowering part of each rep, and add sets when you cannot add weight. Adjustable dumbbells make it much easier.

What should I do if I stop progressing?

Check sleep, protein and calories first. Then drop the weight about 10% and build back up, or swap to a similar exercise for one training block.
